Chromosome Number Evolution in Carnivora

Abhimanyu Arekere

Blackmon Lab · Bioinformatics Lab, Texas A&M University

Abstract

Chromosome number is a fundamental genomic trait, yet the causes of variation in its rate of evolution across lineages remain poorly understood. This project tested whether effective population size, approximated using range size, shapes rates of chromosome fusions and fissions in Carnivora. Using reproducible R workflows and Bayesian phylogenetic modeling, we found that species with small range sizes show elevated rates of chromosome number evolution, supporting the hypothesis that structural rearrangements are mildly deleterious and more likely to fix through genetic drift in smaller populations [2].

The Problem

Across large clades, most lineages show relative stasis in chromosome number while a handful show striking variation. Prior work suggested female meiotic drive shapes karyotype evolution in mammals, but Carnivora shows variation that the meiotic drive model could not adequately explain [2]. We hypothesized that variation in effective population size might underlie this rate variation.

What I Worked On

  • Collected and integrated multiple biological data types: chromosome numbers from karyotype compilations, GBIF species occurrence data, and species-level phylogenies [2].
  • Used the GeoRange R library to estimate species range sizes from longitudinal and latitudinal occurrence data, then validated estimates against previously published range data [2].
  • Helped run reproducible R workflows for downstream comparative analyses, with all scripts version-controlled and reproducible.

Methods

We fit a model of chromosome number evolution to each tree in a posterior distribution of 100 trees, estimating separate rates of fusion and fission for lineages with small versus large range sizes. Models were built using the chromePlus R package and fit using a Bayesian approach in diversitree, with MCMC run for 500 generations across each tree [2]. We also implemented tip-rate reconstruction and a rate heterogeneity analysis to check whether a small number of outlier taxa were driving the results [2].

Key Finding

Rates of both fusions and fissions were elevated in taxa with small range sizes relative to those with large range sizes (ΔRfusion = 0.101, ΔRfission = 0.163, with 95% credible intervals excluding zero) [2]. This supports the conclusion that chromosome-number mutations are mildly deleterious or underdominant, and are more likely to fix via drift in species with small effective population sizes [2].
