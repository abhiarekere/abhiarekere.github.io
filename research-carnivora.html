2020 – 2023

Chromosome Number Evolution in Carnivora

Abhimanyu Arekere

Blackmon Lab · Bioinformatics Lab, Texas A&M University

Abstract

Chromosome number is a fundamental genomic trait, yet the causes of variation in its rate of evolution across lineages remain poorly understood. This research question tested whether effective population size shapes rates of chromosome fusions and fissions in Carnivora. We found that species with small range sizes show elevated rates of chromosome number evolution, providing evidence that structural rearrangements are mildly deleterious and more likely to fix through genetic drift in smaller populations.

The Problem

Prior work suggests that biased female meiotic drive shapes karyotype evolution in mammals, however the variation observed in Carnivora could not be explained by this meiotic drive alone. The hypothesis tested here was that effective population size is that missing factor. Small populations lose the ability to purge slightly deleterious mutations, so structural rearrangements that would be filtered out elsewhere can drift to fixation.

Range size is an imperfect proxy for population size, since density, social structure, and habitat fragmentation all interfere. With this being said, it was chosen because it can be estimated for nearly every species in the clade. It was decided that full taxonomic coverage takes precedence over precision.

What I Worked On

The study drew on three sources that share no common format or identifier scheme: haploid chromosome counts pulled from published karyotype compilations, species occurrence records from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility, and a distribution of 100 candidate phylogenies from a prior study. GBIF alone returned 1,457,081 occurrence records spanning 1,023 species, of which 1,030,610 records across 110 species survived reconciliation with the phylogeny.

Getting to that intersection meant resolving taxonomy across sources, filtering coordinate records, and reshaping everything into the structure the modeling packages expected. I wrote R scripts for that cleaning and integration work, and contributed to the statistical and phylogenetic analysis of the resulting dataset.

Occurrence coordinates were converted into range area estimates using the GeoRange package, and those estimates were checked against independently published range data for 18 species prior to entering comparative analysis. The correlation held at r = 0.61 (n = 18, P = 0.007), which was the signal that the geographic pipeline was sound enough to build on.

Methods

Chromosome number evolution was modeled as two competing processes, fusion and fission, with rates estimated separately for species in small and large range categories. Because range size is continuous and the method requires a binary trait, species were split at the dataset median of roughly 29 million square kilometers. Models were built using the chromePlus R package and fit using a Bayesian approach in diversitree, with MCMC run for 500 generations across each tree.

Three checks guarded against overreading the result. Tip rate reconstruction tested whether a few taxa with extreme karyotypes were dragging the estimate. A rate heterogeneity method assigned every branch in the tree to one of 21 scaled rate bins to locate where fast evolution actually concentrated. And 100 neutral binary traits were simulated and pushed through the same pipeline to measure how often the analysis manufactures a positive result from noise.

Key Finding

Rates of both fusions and fissions were elevated in taxa with small range sizes relative to those with large range sizes (ΔRfusion = 0.101, ΔRfission = 0.163, with 95% credible intervals excluding zero). This supports the conclusion that chromosome-number mutations are mildly deleterious or underdominant, and are more likely to fix via drift in species with small effective population sizes.
