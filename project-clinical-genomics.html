Clinical Genomics Workflow Monitoring

Abhimanyu Arekere

Industry-Sponsored Senior Design Project · UT Austin Department of Biomedical Engineering

Overview

A tumor-informed sequencing assay is only as useful as the speed at which its result reaches a clinician. The biology can be exquisitely sensitive and the answer still arrive too late to change a treatment decision. That gap is an operations problem rather than a molecular one, and it is invisible to the assay itself.

Our team built a lab operations platform that simulates, monitors, and predicts the movement of clinical samples through a high-throughput genomics testing workflow. The system generates realistic synthetic sample data, transforms it into structured analytics products, predicts turnaround time for samples currently in flight, and surfaces bottlenecks, rework, and at-risk cases on a dashboard built for operators to read at a glance.

The Problem

A high-volume clinical lab produces an enormous quantity of operational exhaust: every sample logs a timestamp at every station, and every quality-control failure logs a return to a prior step. The data exists. What did not exist was a way to read it forward. Delays were identified after they had already consumed the turnaround budget, and bottlenecks were diagnosed retrospectively rather than anticipated.

The sponsor's need was a way to model the end-to-end journey of a clinical test sample so that operations staff could see problems developing instead of confirming them afterward.

There was a structural obstacle. Real workflow data is bound to protected health information, which makes it unavailable for a student project and slow to work with even inside the company. That constraint shaped the entire architecture, and it is why the system begins with a simulator rather than a database connection.

What We Built

Workflow Simulator

The assay pipeline is represented as a directed acyclic graph, with nodes as process states and weighted edges as transitions. Samples enter at sample preparation or primer rearray and progress toward sequencing, with quality-control checkpoints that can route a sample backward into rework. Total case duration is drawn from a Gaussian distribution, and the probability that a sample leaves its current node grows with elapsed time according to an exponential holding function. Branching is stochastic: a uniform random draw is compared against accumulated edge weights to select the next state. A dry-run mode validates workflow logic without committing anything to the database.

The result is synthetic data that reproduces the shape of real lab behavior — including the queueing and rework patterns that make turnaround time hard to predict — without touching patient data.

Hybrid Database Architecture

Operational and analytical workloads want opposite things from a database, so we split them. PostgreSQL serves as the transactional store, holding live sample state and transition history for the dashboard to query. Amazon Redshift serves as the analytical warehouse, holding historical data at a volume suited to aggregation and model training. Transformations in both paths are defined in dbt through layered models moving from staging to intermediate to marts and a unified data model, giving the pipeline full lineage from raw event to dashboard tile.

Predictive Model

A linear regression estimates time-to-close for each active case. Features are deliberately austere: site, day of week, hour of case open, and elapsed time from open to DNA extraction. Granular per-step durations were excluded on purpose, because a feature that only exists once a step has finished cannot be used to predict a sample still sitting in that step.     What I Learned

    • Preventing data leakage is a design decision made before any model is trained, not a diagnostic run afterward. The features that would have made our metrics look best were exactly the ones unavailable at prediction time, and excluding them was the difference between a model that reports well and a model that works.
    • A negative result can be more useful than a positive one when it correctly identifies the binding constraint. Learning that more data would not help told us where to spend effort, which a marginally better R² would not have.
    • Splitting hot and cold paths taught me that database architecture is a question about access patterns rather than storage. A dashboard asking for the current state of one sample and a training job aggregating a year of history are different problems, and forcing both through one system compromises each.
    • Building against synthetic data is a legitimate engineering strategy under privacy constraint, and it carries a specific cost: the model learns your assumptions rather than reality. Being explicit about that boundary is what separates a useful prototype from a misleading one.
    • The dashboard iterations changed how I think about output. Our early versions were accurate and unusable. Information that an operator has to hunt for during a shift may as well not have been computed.
