About

Across two summer research experiences in Dr. Alex Keene's neurogenetics lab, I worked on projects connecting genes, neural circuits, sensory behavior, and disease-relevant phenotypes in model organisms including Drosophila and Mexican cavefish. The lab broadly studies how animals sense internal and external environmental changes and adapt behaviors such as sleep, feeding, foraging, and sensory responses.

My primary 2025 project investigated whether Alzheimer's-associated neurodegeneration impairs the olfactory system in Drosophila. I worked with fly models including human APOE4, APOE3, Arctic Aβ42, GLAZ-related crosses, nsyb driver lines, and control backgrounds to evaluate odor-guided behavior. My work also included brain dissections and ablations, tissue mounting, microscopy, immunofluorescence staining of glial cells, behavioral assay design, and computational analysis of biological data.

In 2023, I also ran R-based single-nucleus RNA-seq analysis workflows using tools such as Seurat, Signac, and Monocle3, gaining experience with sequencing-derived neurogenetics datasets and reproducible computational biology workflows.

The Problem

Neurodegenerative disease models can show changes in sensory behavior, but behavioral assays are difficult to interpret when the same animals may also have age-related, starvation-related, or disease-related locomotor deficits. In the olfactory degeneration project, the central question was whether Alzheimer's-associated Drosophila models showed impaired attraction to an odor stimulus, or whether apparent differences in behavior could be explained by movement limitations.

This created a key experimental challenge: the assay needed to measure olfactory response rather than general locomotion. If older, starved, or neurodegenerative flies had difficulty climbing or flying into traps, the experiment could mistakenly measure reduced mobility instead of reduced odor detection.

More broadly, neurogenetics experiments require connecting biological structure, neural function, and measurable behavior. Behavioral results, microscopy images, and sequencing-derived datasets all need to be translated into quantitative, interpretable outputs while controlling for experimental confounders.

Olfactory Degeneration Assay

The main behavioral assay used a trap-based olfaction setup to compare fly attraction toward a low, attractive concentration of acetic acid against a water control. Each flask contained one fly line or treatment condition, with modified microcentrifuge tube traps placed into the assay environment.

I prepared agar solution and poured it into wide-mouth conical flasks while still liquid. After cooling and solidification, I created two indentations in the agar for microcentrifuge tubes: one containing a cotton piece soaked in water as the control, and one containing a cotton piece soaked in an acetic acid solution that I prepared as the odor attractant. The tube caps were modified with pipette tips so flies could enter the trap but could not exit.

Unlike previous setups where tubes were positioned in an empty flask and required flies to climb or fly upward, this modified design placed the tube openings flush with the agar surface. This allowed flies to crawl directly into either the water or acetic acid trap with minimal locomotor demand.

Assays were run across multiple genotypes, ages, and treatment conditions, including APOE4, APOE3, Arctic Aβ42, W118/control lines, DMSO-treated groups, and rotenone-treated groups. Fly age groups included older cohorts such as 5-day, 10-day, and 20-day flies, making locomotor control especially important for interpreting olfactory behavior.

Assay Design & Workflow Improvement

My strongest contribution was modifying the physical design of the olfactory trap assay to reduce locomotor bias. The original style of setup risked confounding odor-guided behavior with the ability of starved, aged, or neurodegenerative flies to climb or fly into elevated traps. By embedding modified microcentrifuge tube traps into agar with the entrances flush to the surface, the assay allowed flies to make a lower-effort odor choice.

This improved the specificity of the behavioral readout by making the assay more focused on olfactory attraction rather than general movement ability. The project strengthened my ability to approach biological experiments as systems that can be measured, modified, troubleshot, and improved.

Sequencing Data Workflow Experience

In addition to behavioral and imaging work, I ran R-based single-nucleus RNA-seq analysis workflows for larval cavefish neurogenetics datasets. These workflows used computational biology tools such as Seurat, Signac, Monocle3, and related R packages to process and analyze high-dimensional sequencing-derived data.

This work involved following established computational workflows, managing R package dependencies, executing scripts, troubleshooting errors, and interpreting outputs from single-cell and single-nucleus analysis tools. It gave me experience with sequencing-adjacent data processing and helped connect wet-lab neurogenetics experiments with computational analysis.

Methods & Workflow

The experimental side of this work involved preparing biological samples, maintaining fly crosses, setting up behavioral assays, preparing odor and control conditions, performing dissections, mounting tissue, conducting microscopy, and running immunofluorescence staining workflows.

The analytical side involved transforming assay, imaging, and sequencing-derived outputs into interpretable data. I used Python, R, Excel, and lab software to analyze experimental results, compare conditions, and support quantitative interpretation of biological phenotypes.

Together, these experiences connected wet-lab experimentation with quantitative analysis and helped me understand how assay design, data quality, workflow reliability, and computational processing influence biological research conclusions.
