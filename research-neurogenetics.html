Summer 2023 & Summer 2025

Neurogenetics Assay Development & Sequencing Data Analysis

Abhimanyu Arekere

Keene Lab · Texas A&M University

About

Across two summers in a neurogenetics lab studying how genes, neural circuits, and internal state shape behavior, I worked with Mexican cavefish and Drosophila model systems. My primary project asked a focused question: does Alzheimer's-associated neurodegeneration impair the olfactory system in Drosophila?

My work spanned brain dissections and ablations, tissue mounting, microscopy, immunofluorescence staining, and behavioral assay design, along with computational analysis of the resulting data. Working with a graduate student, I also ran R-based single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) analysis workflows using tools such as Seurat, Signac, and Monocle3, gaining hands-on exposure to sequencing-derived neurogenetics datasets.

The Problem

Neurodegenerative disease models can alter sensory behavior, but behavioral assays are difficult to interpret when the same animals may also carry age-related or disease-related locomotor deficits. If a fly's movement ability affects its response, an olfactory assay can mistakenly measure impaired locomotion instead of an actual olfactory deficit.

This project used Drosophila Alzheimer's-associated genetic models to test olfactory function while redesigning the assay to reduce that confound. More broadly, the work required connecting biological structure, neural function, and measurable behavior, and translating experimental outputs, such as microscopy images, behavioral results, and sequencing data, into quantitative, interpretable datasets.

Primary Project: Olfactory Behavior in Alzheimer's Models

The central project tested whether Drosophila models of Alzheimer's-associated neurodegeneration show impaired olfactory function. We worked with several genetic backgrounds, including human APOE4 (a late-onset Alzheimer's risk factor), the Arctic (E22G) amyloid-beta line (an aggressive, early-onset familial model), APOE3 as a comparison isoform, and control lines such as w1118 and nsyb-Gal4 drivers.

Behavior was measured using odor trap assays in which flies chose between a water control and a low, attractive concentration of acetic acid. Assays were run across genotype, age (5-, 10-, and 20-day cohorts), and treatment conditions (DMSO vs. rotenone, a stress-inducing agent), allowing us to compare odor-guided behavior across disease models and aging.

Assay Redesign

My main contribution was redesigning the trap assay to isolate olfaction from locomotion. In the original setup, flies had to climb or fly up into elevated tubes to reach the odor, which penalized starved, aged, or neurodegenerative flies with reduced mobility.

I modified the design by embedding modified microcentrifuge-tube traps flush with the surface of an agar-filled flask. Each flask held one water tube and one acetic acid tube, with pipette-tip caps that let flies enter but not exit. Because the tube openings sat level with the agar surface, flies could walk down into a trap with minimal locomotor effort, making the readout far more specific to odor response rather than movement capacity.

Sequencing & Neural Tissue Work

Working alongside a graduate student, I contributed to a second research area involving both wet-lab and computational components. On the wet-lab side, I performed Drosophila brain dissections, tissue mounting, immunofluorescence staining, and microscopy of neural and glial tissue.

On the computational side, I ran R-based snRNA-seq analysis workflows on larval cavefish sequencing datasets using Seurat, Signac, Monocle3, and related packages. This involved package setup, executing and troubleshooting scripts, and interpreting outputs from single-cell and single-nucleus analysis pipelines. This is my primary sequencing-adjacent experience and gave me direct exposure to reproducible computational workflows for high-dimensional sequencing data.

Additional Work: Activity Monitoring

On an occasional basis, I supported Drosophila Activity Monitor (DAM) experiments, helping set up DAMs and work with the associated software for fly lines treated with candidate compounds from other labs to evaluate whether activity phenotypes improved.

Systems Thinking & Approach

This work strengthened my ability to treat biological experiments as systems that can be measured, modified, and improved. The assay redesign followed a clear problem-solving loop: identify a source of bias, re-engineer the experimental setup, and improve the reliability of the readout. The computational work reinforced how experimental design, data quality, and reproducible workflows together determine the reliability of biological conclusions.
